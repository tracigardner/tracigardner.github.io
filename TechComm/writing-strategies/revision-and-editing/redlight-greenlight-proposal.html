Red Light, Green Light

Is Your Proposal Ready?

Give your proposal six quick tests before you submit. A red light means stop and revise. A green light means the document is meeting the goal.

Purposeful?

Does the proposal make a clear case for approving your investigation?

Red Light — Revise

The proposal identifies a topic or document but does not clearly establish what you want approved or why the investigation is worthwhile.

Green Light — Go

The proposal clearly identifies the project, explains why the client document warrants investigation, and supports the request for approval.

Investigative?

Does the proposed program explain how you will research and analyze the client document?

Red Light — Revise

The plan relies on vague tasks such as “research” or “review the document” without explaining what you will examine or how.

Green Light — Go

The plan names specific research and analysis activities and connects them to the communication problems you will investigate.

Research-Supported?

Does preliminary research help justify and guide the proposed investigation?

Red Light — Revise

Sources appear mainly in the annotated bibliography, or research is mentioned without showing how it supports the project.

Green Light — Go

Credible standards, guidelines, research, or professional recommendations help establish what should be investigated and why.

Audience-Centered?

Does the proposal keep the Project Lead, client, and document users clearly in view?

Red Light — Revise

The proposal reads like a class assignment, assumes the audiences are interchangeable, or leaves unclear who needs the eventual recommendations.

Green Light — Go

The proposal addresses the Project Lead professionally and shows how the investigation will serve the client and the people who use its document.

Realistic?

Do your qualifications and schedule show that the project can be completed?

Red Light — Revise

Qualifications are generic, or the Gantt chart is mostly a homework calendar with broad tasks such as research, write, and revise.

Green Light — Go

Relevant qualifications support your role, and the Gantt chart shows specific investigative tasks, timing, duration, sequence, and realistic overlap.

Professional?

Does the finished proposal work as a clear, usable workplace memo?

Red Light — Revise

Dense paragraphs, vague headings, inconsistent design, wordiness, weak visual integration, or accessibility problems make the proposal harder to use.

Green Light — Go

Plain language, useful headings, visual hierarchy, consistent design, accessible presentation, and integrated visuals support quick reading and understanding.

Stop

Before You Go: Check the Basics

Confirm the proposal’s required pieces and submission details before you go.

Proposal memo is 7–9 pages, including the Gantt chart and annotated bibliography.
Required sections are present: memo headers, introduction, proposed program, qualifications and experience, task schedule, and annotated bibliography.
At least five credible outside sources are used and cited in the proposal.
Each annotated bibliography entry includes a citation, summary/use explanation, and two possible quotations.
At least one relevant visual appears in addition to the Gantt chart, with a numbered descriptive caption and a text reference before it appears.
Pages 2 through the end are numbered, formatting is correct, and the final file is saved as a PDF.
