Red Light, Green Light

Is Your Recommendation Report Ready?

Give your recommendation report six quick tests before submitting it. A red light means stop and revise. A green light means the report is meeting the goal.

Investigative?

Are your methods and evaluation criteria clear and credible?

Red Light—Revise

The report describes a general review but does not explain how the client document was examined or what standards guided the evaluation.

Green Light—Go

Methods explain what you examined and how. Explicit criteria grounded in credible research, standards, and guidelines guide the evaluation.

Evidence-Based?

Can readers see the communication problems for themselves?

Red Light—Revise

Findings make broad claims about design, usability, plain language, or accessibility without specific evidence from the client document.

Green Light—Go

Concrete document examples, documented observations, and relevant visuals demonstrate significant problems and their consequences for users.

Analytical?

Do your conclusions explain what the findings mean?

Red Light—Revise

Results, conclusions, and opinions blur together, or conclusions simply repeat observations without explaining their significance.

Green Light—Go

Results present evidence objectively; conclusions interpret the findings, explain consequences for users, and follow logically from the evidence.

Actionable?

Could the client put your recommendations into practice?

Red Light—Revise

Recommendations are vague, disconnected from findings, or impractical—for example, simply saying to make the document more accessible.

Green Light—Go

Specific, feasible improvements address documented problems, explain expected benefits, and consider priorities and implementation.

Audience-Centered?

Does the report help the client make decisions?

Red Light—Revise

The report sounds like a class assignment, overlooks the client’s decision-making needs, or treats the client and document users as the same audience.

Green Light—Go

The report addresses the client professionally, makes the case for improvements, and considers the people who use the document.

Professional?

Does the report model the communication practices it recommends?

Red Light—Revise

Generic headings, dense prose, inconsistent formatting, poorly integrated visuals, or accessibility barriers make the report difficult to use.

Green Light—Go

Informative headings, plain language, accessible visuals, consistent design, and purposeful front and back matter support quick reading and understanding.

Stop

Before You Go: Check the Basics

Confirm the required report elements and submission details before you go.

9–15 pages, including all required content (longer is okay).
Required sections: letter of transmittal, title page, abstract, table of contents, executive summary, introduction, methods, results, conclusions, recommendations, and references.
At least six credible outside sources are meaningfully used and cited, with matching references in your discipline’s documentation style.
At least three relevant visuals have numbered, descriptive captions, are mentioned by number before appearing, and are placed appropriately.
Meaningful hierarchical heading that provide a roadmap to the report.
One-inch margins, correct page numbering, single-spaced paragraphs, double spacing between paragraphs and around headings, and no paragraph indents.
