Integrating Visual Evidence

Make figures and tables part of your explanation—not just something readers look at.

A figure or table can present important evidence, but it cannot make your point on its own. Help readers understand with this four-part technique:

1. IntroducePrepare the reader
2. LabelIdentify the visual
3. ExplainInterpret the evidence
4. ConnectSupport your point

Guidelines for Figures and Tables

  • Number figures and tables separately (Figure 1, Figure 2; Table 1, Table 2).
  • Place figure captions below figures and table titles above tables, unless your required style guide specifies otherwise.
  • Credit sources when needed.
  • Provide accessible descriptions of visual information.

How It Works

Review of the Employee Onboarding Guide

The guide uses similar formatting for headings at different levels. Figure 1 shows an example from the guide.

1. Introduce

Mention the figure before it appears. Tell readers what they are about to see and why it is relevant.

2. Label

Give the figure a number and informative caption. Place figure captions below figures; identify sources when needed.

The heading levels use nearly identical type sizes and weights, making it difficult to distinguish major topics from supporting information.

3. Explain

Point out the pattern, comparison, or detail that matters. Go beyond repeating the caption.

As Figure 1 illustrates, the guide would benefit from more distinct heading styles so readers can locate procedures more easily.

4. Connect

Use the evidence to support a conclusion, recommendation, or other point. Refer to the figure by number.

Notice the connection: The introduction prepares readers for the figure; the caption identifies it; the explanation interprets what it shows; and the final sentence uses that evidence to make a point.

How It Works

The registration page does not clearly distinguish required fields from optional ones. Figure 2 shows the form before revision.

1. Introduce

Identify the interface problem and point readers to the screenshot.

2. Label

Number the screenshot as a figure and caption the feature being examined.

The form provides no visible indication of which fields are required, so users cannot tell what information they must enter.

3. Explain

Describe the specific usability issue visible in the screenshot.

As Figure 2 shows, adding explicit “Required” labels would make the form easier to complete and reduce avoidable errors.

4. Connect

Use the screenshot to justify a practical design improvement.

A Chart, Four Connected Moves

Use a chart to explain a quantitative comparison.

How It Works

Participants took longer to locate instructions in the original guide than in the revised guide. Figure 3 compares the average times.

1. Introduce

Introduce the comparison and direct readers to the chart.

2. Label

Label the chart and make the quantities and units clear.

The average time fell from 72 seconds to 38 seconds, a reduction of 34 seconds in this illustrative comparison.

3. Explain

Interpret the difference rather than simply restating the bars.

Figure 3 suggests that clearer navigation could help readers find instructions more efficiently; actual testing would be needed to confirm the improvement.

4. Connect

Connect the comparison to a qualified conclusion; avoid claiming more than the evidence supports.
