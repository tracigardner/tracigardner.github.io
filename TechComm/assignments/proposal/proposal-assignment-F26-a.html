Proposal
Recommendation Report Project

Your Task

Write a proposal memo requesting approval to investigate one client document and develop a recommendation report for the organization that created it.

You work as a consultant at Caldwell & Associates, a professional organization that helps clients improve their workplace and public-facing communication. Caldwell has received ten documents from ten different clients. Each document presents opportunities to investigate document design and usability, plain language, and accessibility.

Choose one of the ten client documents as the focus of your project. The documents come from different professional contexts so that you can choose something that interests you. You are not required to choose a document connected to your major. Choose the project that you most want to investigate.

Your proposal is the first stage of a larger project. You are not yet writing the recommendation report and you do not need to decide exactly what the client should change. Instead, explain what document you plan to investigate, why the project is worthwhile, how you will conduct the research and analysis, and what resources you expect to use.

Your goal is to persuade your Caldwell supervisor that you have identified a worthwhile project and have a reasonable plan for completing it.

Assignment Learning Objectives

Hide the Learning Objectives Show the Learning Objectives

By completing this assignment, you will develop your ability to:

  • Write a short workplace proposal that requests authorization to proceed with a project.
  • Define the purpose, scope, audience, and expected outcome of a proposed project.
  • Explain the rationale for a project without prematurely reaching conclusions.
  • Develop a practical research method for investigating a workplace communication problem.
  • Identify credible standards, guidelines, and other resources appropriate for professional research.
  • Describe your qualifications for completing a project.
  • Develop a realistic project schedule.
  • Use headings, tables, and other document-design features to make a proposal easy to evaluate.

The Larger Project

The ten client documents come from different professional settings, but your role as a Caldwell consultant is essentially the same regardless of which document you choose.

In the later recommendation report, you will investigate how effectively the document communicates with its intended audience and recommend standards or guidelines that the client organization can use to improve this document and future documents.

  • Document Design & Usability: How effectively does the document organize and present information so readers can find, understand, and use it?
  • Plain Language: How effectively does the document communicate information at an appropriate level of complexity for its audience and purpose?
  • Accessibility: How effectively does the document support access for readers with different abilities, technologies, and ways of interacting with information?

The proposal does not need to provide your complete analysis. Instead, show that the document presents a meaningful communication problem and that you have a reasonable plan for investigating it.

Your Audience

Write your proposal to your supervisor at Caldwell & Associates, who must approve your project before you begin the full recommendation report.

Your supervisor needs enough information to determine:

  • what client document you have selected;
  • what communication problem you intend to investigate;
  • why the project deserves Caldwell’s time;
  • how you will research and analyze the problem;
  • who ultimately needs the recommendations;
  • whether you are prepared to complete the work; and
  • whether your timetable is realistic.

Remember the difference between the proposal audience and the project audience. Your Caldwell supervisor reads the proposal and authorizes your work. The client organization that created your selected document is the audience for the recommendation report you are proposing.

Assignment Requirements

Basic Requirements

  • Genre: Workplace proposal formatted as a professional memo.
  • Length: 2–3 pages, including the timetable.
  • Organization: Follow the eight required sections below. Use headings for Sections 3–7. The introduction and conclusion should remain unheaded.
  • Tone: Professional, confident, specific, and forward-looking.
  • Evidence: Refer specifically to your selected client document when explaining the rationale for the project.
  • Plain Language: Write concisely and directly. Avoid unnecessary academic or inflated proposal language.
  • Accessibility: Use meaningful headings, accessible table structure, sufficient contrast, descriptive links when links are included, and other appropriate accessible-document practices.
  • Submission: Submit the completed proposal as a PDF.

Section 1: Memo Headers

Begin with standard memo headers:

To: [Caldwell supervisor]
From: [Your name], Consultant, Caldwell & Associates
Date: [Submission date]
Subject: Proposal to Evaluate [brief identification of client document]

Make the subject line specific enough that your reader can identify the proposed project immediately.

Do not add a heading labeled “Memo Headers.”

Section 2: Introduction

Begin with a short introductory paragraph that establishes the purpose of the memo.

Identify:

  • the client organization;
  • the document you propose to analyze;
  • the general purpose and audience of that document; and
  • your request for approval to proceed with the project.

Keep this section brief. Your reader should know what you are proposing by the end of the introduction.

Do not add an “Introduction” heading.

Section 3: Overview and Rationale

Explain the proposed project in more detail. Briefly describe the client document, including what it is intended to accomplish and how its audience is expected to use it.

Then explain why the document is worth investigating. Identify preliminary concerns or questions involving:

  • document design and usability;
  • plain language; and
  • accessibility.

Use enough specific evidence from the document to show that your proposed investigation has a reasonable basis. You might notice, for example, that a table is difficult to interpret, terminology may not suit the intended audience, information may be hard to locate, or a visual feature raises an accessibility question.

At this stage, these are areas for investigation rather than final conclusions. Do not turn this section into the recommendation report.

Section 4: Methods and Resources

Explain how you will conduct the project. Your methods should include both analysis of the client document and research into appropriate communication standards.

Explain how you plan to:

  • analyze organization, hierarchy, layout, tables, visuals, navigation, and other relevant design features;
  • evaluate the document’s language in relation to its audience and purpose;
  • investigate accessibility issues relevant to the document;
  • research authoritative standards, guidelines, or professional recommendations; and
  • compare those standards with specific features of the client document.

Identify specific resources you expect to consult whenever possible. Depending on your project, useful resources might include government guidance, professional organizations, accessibility standards, plain-language guidance, usability research, disciplinary standards, or other credible sources.

You do not need to have completed the research yet. You do need to show that you know where and how you intend to look for reliable evidence.

Avoid a research method that amounts to “I will search Google and find sources.”

Section 5: Project Audience

Analyze the audience for the recommendation report you are proposing.

Identify the people within the client organization who would be able to evaluate or implement your recommendations. Consider:

  • what responsibilities they have;
  • what they probably already know about the document and its subject;
  • what they need from Caldwell;
  • what constraints may affect their decisions;
  • what kinds of evidence will make recommendations credible to them; and
  • how they might use the final report.

Also consider the users of the client document. Even when those users will not read your recommendation report, their needs should influence the standards you eventually recommend.

Avoid describing an audience only with demographics. Focus primarily on what readers know, need, expect, and will do with the information.

Section 6: Personnel & Qualifications

Identify yourself as the Caldwell consultant responsible for the project and explain why you are prepared to complete the work.

Relevant qualifications can include:

  • coursework;
  • disciplinary knowledge;
  • workplace or volunteer experience;
  • experience with technical or professional communication;
  • familiarity with the document’s subject;
  • research experience;
  • knowledge of document design, plain language, or accessibility; and
  • skills you have developed in this course.

You do not need to be an expert in the client’s discipline.

If you choose a project outside your major, explain how your communication and research skills prepare you to learn what you need. If you choose something related to your major, explain how that disciplinary knowledge will help without assuming that subject expertise automatically makes you an expert communicator.

Do not invent credentials.

Section 7: Timetable

Provide a table showing the major stages of your proposed work and when you expect to complete them.

Include activities such as:

  • reviewing the client document;
  • researching relevant standards and guidelines;
  • analyzing document design and usability;
  • analyzing plain language;
  • analyzing accessibility;
  • developing recommendations;
  • drafting the recommendation report;
  • reviewing and revising the report; and
  • submitting the final report.

Combine activities when appropriate. Your timetable should represent a realistic work plan rather than a list created simply to fill the section.

Use the actual course deadlines when they are available, and allow time for research, analysis, drafting, and revision.

Section 8: Conclusion

End with a short paragraph that:

  • reinforces the value of the proposed project;
  • confirms that you are prepared to proceed according to the plan; and
  • directly requests approval to move forward with the research and recommendation report.

Do not introduce new project details in the conclusion.

Do not add a “Conclusion” heading.

Success Tips

  • Choose based on interest, not your major. All ten projects are designed to support the same recommendation-report assignment.
  • Propose an investigation rather than announcing your conclusions. Your proposal should identify questions and preliminary concerns, not present a finished recommendation report.
  • Be specific about your chosen document. This assignment page is generic; your proposal should not be.
  • Separate symptoms from standards. A confusing link, table, paragraph, or heading may point toward a larger organizational communication problem.
  • Think beyond correcting one document. Your later report should recommend standards or guidelines the client can apply to future documents.
  • Make your research plan credible. Identify the kinds of authoritative guidance you expect to use rather than promising vaguely to “do research.”
  • Use the timetable as a planning tool. Build in time to analyze evidence and revise, not just research and write.

Submission Details

  • Save your proposal as a PDF so that your document formatting remains stable in Canvas.
  • Upload the final PDF to this assignment page.

Avoid these common errors

  • Don’t email your assignment. I grade all work in Canvas.
  • Don’t submit a link in place of the PDF.
  • Don’t write the recommendation report yet. This assignment proposes the project.
  • Don’t invent professional qualifications or research you have not completed.
