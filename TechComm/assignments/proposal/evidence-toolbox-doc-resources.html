Evidence Toolbox

Accessibility

Use trusted standards and guidance to evaluate how well a document works for people with different abilities and technologies.

Start Here When You Need to Ask:

Use accessibility resources when your investigation asks questions such as:

  • Does the document meet established accessibility expectations?
  • What barriers might some users encounter?
  • What specific criteria can support an accessibility recommendation?
Microsoft

Make Your Word Documents Accessible

Microsoft’s Word-specific guide explains common accessibility problems and shows how to check headings, images, tables, links, color, and other document features.

Use It For

  • Running Word’s built-in Accessibility Checker.
  • Checking headings, alt text, tables, links, and color.
  • Understanding how to fix common accessibility problems in Word.
Open Microsoft’s Word Guide
WebAIM

Word Accessibility Evaluation Guide

WebAIM combines Word’s automated accessibility check with straightforward manual checks for issues the software may not identify on its own.

Use It For

  • Evaluating an existing Word document systematically.
  • Checking headings, images, contrast, tables, lists, and document properties.
  • Knowing what requires a manual check in addition to Word’s checker.
Use the WebAIM Evaluation Guide
Section 508.gov

Accessible Documents: Microsoft Word

Section508.gov collects Word-specific authoring and testing guides, including current resources for Microsoft Word 365.

Use It For

  • Following a structured Word accessibility review.
  • Checking whether a document meets federal accessibility expectations.
  • Finding Word-specific guides and testing resources in one place.
Browse Word Accessibility Guides

Start with the Word Document Itself

Run Word’s Accessibility Checker, then use a manual guide to review features the checker may miss. Describe the specific problem you find and connect it to guidance you can cite.

Plain Language

Use established guidance and assessment tools to evaluate whether readers can find, understand, and use the information they need.

Start Here When You Need to Ask:

Use plain-language resources when your investigation asks questions such as:

  • Is the document organized around what readers need?
  • Are wording, sentences, headings, and layout easy to understand?
  • Can readers quickly identify the main message or required action?
Digital.gov

Plain Language Guide

Digital.gov explains how to write, design, and test content so a specific audience can understand it.

Use It For

  • Evaluating audience focus and organization.
  • Checking clear wording and reader-centered writing.
  • Examining how design supports understanding.
Explore the Plain Language Guide
CDC

Clear Communication Index

The CDC Index is a research-based tool for developing and assessing public communication materials.

Use It For

  • Evaluating the main message and call to action.
  • Checking plain language and information design.
  • Using a structured assessment rather than intuition alone.
Use the CDC Index
National Archives

Plain Writing Checklist

The National Archives checklist provides a concise review of audience, organization, wording, sentences, headings, lists, tables, and white space.

Use It For

  • Running a quick plain-language review.
  • Checking organization and sentence-level clarity.
  • Evaluating readability features such as headings and white space.
Open the Checklist

Evidence Toolbox

Usability

Use research-based principles and user-centered methods to evaluate whether people can find information, understand what to do, and complete their tasks successfully.

Start Here When You Need to Ask:

Use usability resources when your investigation asks questions such as:

  • Can users find what they need without unnecessary effort?
  • Does the document match users’ expectations and tasks?
  • What would you need to observe or test with actual users?
CMS

Toolkit for Testing Written Material with Readers

CMS developed this nontechnical toolkit specifically for people without formal research training who need to collect feedback on written materials.

Use It For

  • Planning a simple reader-feedback or usability session.
  • Giving readers realistic tasks to complete with a document.
  • Watching where readers struggle, misunderstand, or miss information.
Open the CMS Written-Materials Toolkit
GOV.UK

Usability Testing: Qualitative Studies

GOV.UK explains a simple task-based approach: give representative users something realistic to do, define what success looks like, and observe what happens.

Use It For

  • Creating realistic find-and-use tasks for a Word document.
  • Defining simple success criteria before testing.
  • Observing where users hesitate, get lost, or fail to complete a task.
Read the Usability Testing Guide
User Research

Tips for Testing Your Words

This practical government guide shows how to test whether readers understand wording, find the right information, and know what to do next—including with a printed document.

Use It For

  • Asking readers to explain information in their own words.
  • Having readers mark confusing or especially useful passages.
  • Checking whether readers know what action to take after finding information.
Read the Content-Testing Tips

Test What Readers Can Find, Understand, and Do

For a Word document, usability does not require a complicated lab. Give a reader a realistic task, observe what happens, and record where the document helps or gets in the way.
