Evidence Toolbox

Look Beyond the Document

Find Evidence You Can Use to Evaluate the Client Document

Outside sources give you criteria for deciding whether a communication choice is effective. Instead of searching generally for document design or accessibility, start with established standards, research-based guidance, and models from organizations that publish their practices.

Resources to Reach For

Upper Tray: Outside Evidence
1

Accessibility Standards & Guidance

Use these sources when you need criteria for evaluating digital accessibility, document structure, images, contrast, navigation, or other barriers to access.

W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 The current W3C accessibility standard. Use the success criteria when you need specific, testable accessibility requirements. w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ W3C Web Accessibility Initiative Tutorials Practical explanations and examples for accessible images, tables, page structure, forms, menus, and related features. w3.org/WAI/tutorials/ Section 508 ICT Testing Baselines U.S. government testing criteria for evaluating accessibility requirements in electronic documents, web content, software, and hardware. section508.gov
2

Plain Language & Clear Communication

Use these sources when you are investigating clarity, organization, wording, headings, reader focus, or information density.

Digital.gov Plain Language Guide Federal guidance on audience, organization, word choice, clear writing, readable design, and testing content. digital.gov/guides/plain-language/ National Archives Plain Writing Checklist A concise checklist for reviewing audience awareness, organization, wording, sentence style, headings, white space, lists, tables, and other readability features. archives.gov/open/plain-writing/checklist CDC Clear Communication Index A research-based assessment tool for evaluating public communication materials, including main messages, plain language, information design, numbers, and calls to action. cdc.gov/ccindex/
3

Usability Principles

Use these when you need to evaluate whether readers can find information, understand what to do, move through a document, or complete a task efficiently.

Nielsen Norman Group: 10 Usability Heuristics Established principles for evaluating consistency, user control, error prevention, recognition, efficiency, minimalist design, and help/documentation. nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/ U.S. Web Design System: Design Principles User-centered principles addressing real user needs, accessibility, continuity, trust, simplicity, and effective digital experiences. designsystem.digital.gov/design-principles/
4

Design Models & Comparison Documents

Use model systems to see how experienced organizations solve recurring design and usability problems. Treat them as comparison evidence, not templates you must copy.

U.S. Web Design System (USWDS) Browse accessible components, patterns, typography, design principles, and implementation guidance used by U.S. federal digital services. designsystem.digital.gov/ GOV.UK Design System Browse tested styles, components, and patterns with explanations of when and how they should be used for user-focused services. design-system.service.gov.uk/
5

Professional Writing & Style Guidance

Use professional style guides when the client document includes technical explanations, procedures, interface text, or other specialized workplace communication.

Microsoft Writing Style Guide Guidance for clear technical and digital communication, including terminology, instructions, word choice, interface text, accessibility, and bias-free communication. learn.microsoft.com/style-guide/
For discipline-specific guidance Search the professional association, government agency, standards body, or scholarly database connected to the client document’s subject. Look for terms such as guidelines, standards, best practices, style guide, or design system. Some formal industry standards are not freely available; your university library may provide access.
Use sources as criteria, not decoration. A source is useful when it helps you explain what you will examine, what effective communication should do, or how you will judge what you find.

Turn a Source into an Investigation Tool

1

Identify the Issue

Example: Users may have trouble locating procedures because headings do not clearly describe the content beneath them.

2

Find Relevant Guidance

Locate credible guidance about descriptive headings, information organization, navigation, or scanning.

3

Use It as a Criterion

Compare the client document with the guidance and explain what features you will examine in the recommendation report.
