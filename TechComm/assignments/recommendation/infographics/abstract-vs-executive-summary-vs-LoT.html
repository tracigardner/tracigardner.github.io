Purpose & Audience
in Your Front Matter

Three parts of a recommendation report's front matter typically mention the same project, but they serve different readers and different purposes. Choose what to emphasize based on what each reader needs.

Letter of Transmittal

What report are you sending me, and why?

Abstract

Is this technical report relevant to me?

Executive Summary

What does this mean for our organization, and what should we do?

Compare the Three

Compare Letter of Transmittal Abstract Executive Summary
Audience The primary recipient of the report, typically the client contact or person who commissioned it. Readers familiar with the technical subject, such as researchers and specialists. Managers and decision-makers who need to understand the project's organizational significance.
Purpose Introduce and deliver the report while establishing a courteous, professional relationship with its recipient. Help readers decide whether to read the full report. Help managers decide what the findings mean and what action to take.
Content Identify the enclosed report and its purpose and content; often note who authorized the work and acknowledge assistance. May briefly mention a key finding or recommendation. Technical background, purpose, methods, and—if informative—major findings and recommendations. May include searchable keywords. Problem or opportunity, major findings and implications, conclusions, and recommendations. Methods are usually only a sentence or two.
Level of detail Brief, reader-directed correspondence. Enough context to introduce the report, not a substitute for its summaries. Usually no more than 200 words. Concise, but technical terminology is appropriate. Typically about one double-spaced page for a report under 20 pages. Prioritizes concrete evidence, consequences, and decisions over technical detail.
Form and tone Letter, memo, or email. Direct address, professional greeting and closing, courteous tone. Compact technical summary, often one paragraph, with optional keywords. Self-contained, decision-oriented summary written for management.

Same Project, Three Different Jobs

Imagine a consultant evaluating an engineering firm's employee onboarding guide. Notice how the same investigation is presented to three audiences.

Letter of Transmittal

Focus: Deliver the report

Dear Engineering Manager:

Enclosed is our report evaluating the usability and accessibility of your employee onboarding guide, prepared in response to your request. It outlines our review process, findings, and recommended improvements to navigation and terminology.

Thank you for the opportunity to complete this review. We would be happy to discuss the recommendations with your team.

Sincerely,
Maroon + Orange Digital

Abstract

Focus: Technical evaluation

This report evaluates the usability and accessibility of an engineering onboarding guide using a document-design review, a plain-language assessment, and a task-based navigation test. The evaluation identified inconsistent heading levels, undefined technical terminology, and difficulty locating fieldwork procedures. The report recommends a revised heading hierarchy, a glossary of recurring terms, and task-oriented navigation aids.

Keywords: onboarding documentation; usability; accessibility; document design; plain language

Executive Summary

Focus: Client decision and action

New engineers need to locate workplace procedures quickly during onboarding and daily work. Our review found that the current guide makes several important procedures difficult to find and introduces terminology without explanation. In a navigation test, four of six participants could not locate the fieldwork checklist within two minutes.

We recommend prioritizing a clearer heading structure, direct links to frequently used procedures, and explanations of essential terminology. These changes would make the guide easier to use without requiring the company to rewrite its technical policies. The client should implement the navigation changes first, then test the revised guide with new employees before the next onboarding cycle.

Note on examples: The project, correspondence, research activities, and findings above are fictional teaching examples.

Two Types of Abstracts

Descriptive Abstract

Identifies the topics covered without presenting major findings or recommendations.

Informative Abstract

Summarizes the investigation's major findings and recommendations.

Use this type for your recommendation report.

Remember the Difference

Letter of Transmittal = professional delivery and introduction. Abstract = technical relevance. Executive Summary = managerial meaning and action. Some information overlaps, but each section has a distinct job.
