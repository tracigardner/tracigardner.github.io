Subjective Writing vs. Objective Writing

Many workplace documents—like informational reports and lab reports—should inform, not persuade. Objective writing presents neutral facts so readers can make their own decisions.

Aspect Subjective Writing Objective Writing
Purpose Persuades or influences the reader to agree with the writer. Informs the reader so they can understand the options and make their own decisions.
Focus Writer’s opinions, preferences, judgments, or experiences. Facts, features, functions, capabilities, and limitations of the topic or tools.
Tone

Emotional, opinionated, promotional, or evaluative.

Example: “This tool is the best choice for your needs.”

Neutral, professional, and unbiased.

Example: “This tool offers features that may meet your needs.”

Language
to Avoid
  • best, greatest, amazing
  • I believe, I think
  • you should, I recommend
  • in my opinion
  • superior to, better than
 Instead, use neutral language such as:
  • provides, offers, includes
  • can, may, is designed to
  • supports, allows, enables
  • according to, based on
  • similar to, different from
Language
to Use 		Loaded or evaluative words that reveal the writer’s judgment or bias. Specific, measurable, and verifiable information supported by credible sources.
Examples Subjective statement:

“Tool A is easier to use and much more reliable than Tool B.”

Objective statement:

“Tool A reports a 99.9% uptime rate, while Tool B reports a 98.5% uptime rate.”

Impact on
the Reader 		May pressure or sway the reader and reduce trust in the document. Respects the reader’s ability to evaluate information and make an independent decision.
