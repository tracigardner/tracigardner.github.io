Integrating Sources

Make research part of your reasoning—not just something you cite.

Research strengthens a report when readers understand what a source contributes and why it matters. Use this four-step technique to incorporate evidence into your own explanation:

1. IntroducePrepare the reader
2. IntegratePresent the evidence
3. ExplainInterpret the evidence
4. ConnectSupport your point

The four steps may occur within a single sentence or across several sentences.

Summary in a Sample Document

Condense the main findings of a source without repeating every detail. A summary is shorter and broader than the original.

Sample Source

Usability review notes: In a review of 12 university department webpages, reviewers identified inconsistent navigation labels, missing contact information, and confusing links to application instructions. These problems appeared across multiple pages.

Summary in a Sample Report

How It Works

The department website needs more consistent navigation. An internal usability review provides an overview of the problems across its pages.

1. Introduce

Establish the source and explain why readers need this evidence.

The review found recurring problems with navigation labels, contact details, and links to application instructions [1, pp. 3–4].

2. Integrate

Incorporate the source material accurately and identify where the information comes from.

Together, these findings suggest that visitors encounter several obstacles when trying to locate routine information.

3. Explain

Interpret the information rather than leaving it to speak for itself.

The department should prioritize a sitewide review of navigation and contact information rather than correcting only one page.

4. Connect

Show how the evidence supports your evaluation or recommendation.

Paraphrase in a Sample Document

Restate a specific idea or finding in your own words and sentence structure while preserving its meaning.

Sample Source

Accessibility review notes: Several application form fields rely on placeholder text alone to identify the information users must enter. When users begin typing, the placeholder disappears, making it harder to confirm what each field requires.

Paraphrase in a Sample Report

How It Works

The application form may create unnecessary difficulties for users. The accessibility review identifies a specific issue with field instructions.

1. Introduce

Establish the source and explain why readers need this evidence.

According to the accessibility review, some fields use disappearing placeholder text instead of persistent labels, leaving users without visible guidance once they begin entering information [2, p. 6].

2. Integrate

Incorporate the source material accurately and identify where the information comes from.

This design makes it harder for applicants to check that they are completing the correct fields.

3. Explain

Interpret the information rather than leaving it to speak for itself.

The department should add persistent, clearly associated labels to its application form fields.

4. Connect

Show how the evidence supports your evaluation or recommendation.

Quotation in a Sample Document

Use a source’s exact words when the particular wording matters. Quote selectively and make clear whose words they are.

Sample Source

Student interview: “I kept going back to the homepage because I couldn’t tell which link had the application instructions.”

Quotation in a Sample Report

How It Works

A student interview illustrates the difficulty of locating application instructions.

1. Introduce

Establish the source and explain why readers need this evidence.

One participant explained, “I kept going back to the homepage because I couldn’t tell which link had the application instructions” [3, p. 2].

2. Integrate

Incorporate the source material accurately and identify where the information comes from.

The participant’s description points to unclear link labels and repeated navigation rather than a lack of interest in applying.

3. Explain

Interpret the information rather than leaving it to speak for itself.

More descriptive link text and a clearly marked application pathway could reduce this confusion.

4. Connect

Show how the evidence supports your evaluation or recommendation.

Synthesis in a Sample Document

Bring findings from multiple sources together to establish a larger pattern or insight. Do more than list sources one after another.

Sample Source

Usability review notes: Reviewers found inconsistent navigation labels on 8 of 12 department webpages.

Student interview notes: Several students reported difficulty predicting where application-related links would lead.

Support request log: The department received repeated questions asking where to find application instructions.

Synthesis in a Sample Report

How It Works

Several sources suggest that applicants struggle to find information on the department website.

1. Introduce

Establish the source and explain why readers need this evidence.

  • The usability review identified inconsistent labels on 8 of 12 pages [1, p. 3].
  • Student interviews described uncertainty about link destinations [3, pp. 2–3].
  • The support log recorded repeated requests for application directions [4, p. 5].

2. Integrate

Incorporate the source material accurately and identify where the information comes from.

These different forms of evidence point to the same underlying problem: the site does not provide a predictable route to application information.

3. Explain

Interpret the information rather than leaving it to speak for itself.

A consistent navigation system with clearly named application links would address a problem documented by reviewers, students, and support staff.

4. Connect

Show how the evidence supports your evaluation or recommendation.

Comparison in a Sample Document

Place sources in conversation by showing where their findings agree, differ, or qualify one another.

Sample Source

Staff review notes: Staff reviewers reported that the application link was easy to find from the admissions landing page.

Student interview notes: Students entering through program pages often struggled to locate the application link.

Comparison in a Sample Report

How It Works

Staff and student assessments of the application navigation differ, so the entry point matters when evaluating the site.

1. Introduce

Establish the source and explain why readers need this evidence.

Staff reviewers found the application link easy to locate from the admissions landing page [5, p. 2]. In contrast, students who started on program pages often had difficulty finding it [3, p. 4].

2. Integrate

Incorporate the source material accurately and identify where the information comes from.

The findings are not necessarily contradictory: the navigation may work well from one page while remaining unclear from other common entry points.

3. Explain

Interpret the information rather than leaving it to speak for itself.

The department should test and improve application links across program pages, not just on the admissions landing page.

4. Connect

Show how the evidence supports your evaluation or recommendation.

Remember: Summaries, paraphrases, quotations, and ideas drawn from multiple sources all require attribution. IEEE citations use bracketed numbers that correspond to numbered entries in a reference list; include page numbers when pointing to a specific passage. In your writing, use your discipline’s documentation style (e.g., engineers use IEEE, biologists use CSE).
