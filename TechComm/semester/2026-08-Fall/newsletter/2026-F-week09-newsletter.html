Your Sources Should Leave the Bibliography

Your annotated bibliography shows that you found credible sources. But your research also needs to do some work in the proposal itself.

Use preliminary research where it helps explain why an issue deserves investigation or what standards, guidelines, and methods can guide your later evaluation.

Bring Research Into the Proposal

  • Use a standard to show what an accessible document should provide.
  • Use professional guidance to establish a plain-language or usability principle.
  • Use a research method to explain how you could evaluate the client document.
  • Cite the source where you use the information.

That does not mean proving your eventual findings now. Preliminary research helps justify and shape the investigation; the full analysis comes later.

If all five sources appear only in the annotated bibliography, they aren’t yet helping you make the case for your project.

Let your sources help the Project Lead understand why the investigation is worthwhile and how you plan to approach it.

Do a Final “So What?” Pass

A proposal can explain what you plan to do without making clear why that work matters.

During revision, look for sentences that describe an activity and ask yourself: So what?

Push the Reasoning One Step Further

  • I will evaluate the headings.
    Why do the headings matter to the document’s users?
  • I will examine accessibility.
    What could accessibility barriers prevent readers from doing?
  • I will conduct a usability test.
    What will that test help you learn about how readers use the document?

You don’t need to make every sentence longer. You do need to make sure the proposal explains the value of the work you’re asking permission to do.

Don’t stop at “Here’s what I’ll do.” Show the reader why doing it will matter.

A strong proposal gives the Project Lead reasons to approve the investigation, not just a list of activities.

See Your Proposal as an Outline

When you’ve been reading the same paragraphs for days, the sentences can make it difficult to see the document’s structure.

Microsoft Word and Google Docs both provide ways to view your headings separately from most of the body text. That gives you a quick outline of the document you actually wrote.

How to Read the Headings Only in Your Word Processor

In Microsoft Word
  • Use the Navigation Pane or Outline view to see headings together.
  • Find more details in the Microsoft documentation for the Navigation pane.
In Google Docs
  • Open the document outline to see headings in a separate panel.
  • Find more details in the Google Documentation for Viewing document outlines.

Then ignore the paragraphs for a moment. Read the headings from top to bottom and ask whether you can follow the proposal’s logic, identify its major sections, and see how subsections fit together.

This view can expose vague headings, inconsistent heading levels, sections that seem out of order, and long stretches of text without useful signposts.

Think of the headings-only view as an X-ray: it lets you inspect the document’s structure without the sentences getting in the way.

If the outline doesn’t make sense on its own, revise the headings or organization until the roadmap is clear.
