Use Your Sources

Your annotated bibliography shows what you found. Now make sure some of that research is doing work in the proposal itself.

Use your research in the proposal to explain

  • why something warrants investigation.
  • what criteria could guide your evaluation.
  • how you could investigate the client document.
If all five sources live only in the annotated bibliography, your research isn’t yet helping you make the case for your project.

Do a Final “So What?” Pass

A proposal can explain what you plan to do without explaining why that work matters.

Don’t stop at listing activities. Go on to state why the activity is worth doing—to answer the question, “So what?”

Evaluate the headings. Why do the headings matter to users?

See Your Proposal as an Outline

When you’ve been reading the same draft for days, it’s hard to see its structure. Hide the paragraphs and look at the headings instead. It’s easy to do in your word processor.

Display the Headings Only in Your Word Processor

In Microsoft Word
  • Use the Navigation Pane or Outline view to see headings together.
  • Find more details in the Microsoft documentation for the Navigation pane.
In Google Docs
  • Open the document outline to see headings in a separate panel.
  • Find more details in the Google documentation for viewing document outlines.

Once you can see the headings together, read them from top to bottom. Based only on that outline, answer these questions:

  • What are the major sections of the proposal?
  • How do the sections of the proposal fit together?
  • Where would you look in the proposal for specific information?

If the headings don’t provide the answers, revise them to make the document’s structure and navigation clearer. If you need help, consult Headings: A Roadmap for Your Reader.
