You Read Technical Writing Every Day

You may be new to writing technical communication, but you’re definitely not new to reading it.

Think about some of the information you encounter in an ordinary week:

  • A weather alert tells you whether you need to change your plans.
  • A map helps you find an unfamiliar building.
  • An FAQ answers a question about a service.
  • A medication label tells you how much to take and warns you about risks.
  • A troubleshooting message tells you what went wrong and what to try next.
  • A nutrition chart helps you compare choices.
  • An online form tells you what information you need to provide.

These documents may look completely different, but they have something important in common: they help people use information to accomplish something.

That’s one reason technical communication is broader than simply “writing about technology.” Technical communicators create information for clients, coworkers, managers, specialists, users, and the public.

Technical communication can help people understand information, make decisions, or take action.

Try It This Week

Start noticing the technical communication around you. When you encounter a particularly good—or particularly frustrating—example, ask yourself:

  • Who is the audience?
  • What does that person need to do?
  • How well does the document help them do it?

You’ve just started analyzing technical communication.

Your Reader Is Probably Not Reading Every Word

Here’s an uncomfortable truth about workplace writing: Your reader may not read everything you write.

That doesn’t necessarily mean your writing is boring or unimportant. It means workplace readers often approach documents differently from the way you might read a novel or an essay.

Workplace readers may:

  • Scan a document for a particular heading.
  • Skim to identify the main ideas.
  • Jump directly to a table, list, or section that answers their question.

Imagine opening a 20-page report because you need to know one thing: How much will the proposed project cost? You probably aren’t going to start on page one and carefully read every sentence until you find the answer.

Good technical communication anticipates this behavior.

That’s why technical documents use features such as:

Descriptive headings Short paragraphs Lists Tables Visuals Captions White space

These features aren’t just decoration. They help readers understand the document and find the information they need.

Make the information easy to find—even for the reader who is in a hurry.
