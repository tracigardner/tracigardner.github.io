Don’t Start on Page One

Your recommendation report begins with a letter of transmittal, title page, abstract, and other front matter. But that doesn’t mean you have to write those sections first. In fact, it’s a bad idea to start there!

Start with the parts you can work on now:

  • Introduction: Explain the document and the communication issues you’re investigating.
  • Methods: Describe how you’re evaluating the document.
  • Results: Record findings as your investigation produces evidence.

Write the executive summary and abstract later, when you know what the report says.

The first page readers see doesn’t have to be the first page you write.

Keep an Evidence Log

As you investigate your client’s document, you’ll notice problems, find useful examples, and connect your observations to professional standards. Don’t rely on memory to keep track of everything.

Create a simple table to record evidence as you work. For example:

Example Evidence Log
Issue Evidence to Record
Headings Examples of headings that may not help readers locate information.
Plain language Passages to compare against plain-language guidelines.
Accessibility Specific features to check against accessibility standards.

Include page numbers, screenshots, and source references so you can find the evidence again.

Your evidence log can become the foundation for your Results section.
