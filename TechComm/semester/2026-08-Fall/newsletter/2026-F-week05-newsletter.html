🧩Make Related Things Look Related

Readers shouldn’t have to guess which pieces of information belong together.

🔁 Repeated colors, heading styles, icons, spacing, and layouts can show that several items have the same role or level of importance.

📐 Alignment and proximity help too. Put related information close together and line up similar elements so readers can see the connections.

For your learning aid, look at the sections that explain similar concepts. Do they follow a consistent pattern, or does every section look completely different?

Good design creates patterns readers can recognize. Consistency helps them understand the structure without having to stop and figure it out.

📄Don’t Shrink It—Edit It

Your learning aid has to fit on one page. If it doesn’t, your first instinct may be to make everything smaller.

🔎 But tiny text and cramped spacing solve a page problem by creating a readability problem.

✂️ Before shrinking anything, cut repetition, shorten long explanations, combine related ideas, and remove information your reader doesn’t really need.

You can also let lists, tables, diagrams, or other useful visuals carry information more efficiently than another paragraph.

If the page is still overloaded, you may simply be trying to cover too many concepts.

When the page is too full, make the content smarter before you make the type smaller.

🚧A Rough Draft Should Actually Be Rough

A rough draft isn’t supposed to look finished. It’s supposed to give you something substantial to test and improve.

🛠️ By the rough-draft checkpoint, you should have enough content and design in place to see whether your ideas actually work together on the page.

👀 Look for problems while they’re still easy to change: crowded sections, confusing organization, weak visuals, missing information, or areas that take too much space.

Don’t spend all your time polishing tiny details in a section you may later cut or redesign.

The goal of a rough draft isn’t perfection. It’s to give yourself enough of a document to make meaningful revision decisions.

🧪Can Someone Learn From This Without You?

You already understand your topic. Your reader may not.

🙋 That makes it easy to leave out explanations or connections that seem obvious to you but may not be obvious to a non-expert reader.

👥 Try giving your learning aid to someone unfamiliar with the topic. Don’t explain it first. Let the document do the work.

Can they understand the concepts? Can they tell why the ideas matter? Can they recognize examples and figure out how to apply what they learned?

If they get confused, that confusion gives you useful information about what to revise.

A successful user document shouldn’t need its author standing beside it. The page itself should teach the reader what they need to know.
