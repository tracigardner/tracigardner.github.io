What Counts as Evidence in a Proposal?

You haven’t completed your investigation yet, so your proposal shouldn’t try to prove what’s wrong with the client document.

Instead, use preliminary evidence to show that the project is worth investigating and that you have a reasonable way to investigate it.

Useful Proposal Evidence

  • Observations from the client document that identify possible issues worth examining.
  • Professional standards and guidelines that establish criteria you could use in your evaluation.
  • Credible research that explains why issues such as usability, accessibility, plain language, or document design matter.
  • Research methods or assessment tools that give you a practical way to investigate the document later.

Notice what’s missing from that list: findings. You aren’t expected to know yet whether a possible problem is significant or what should be done about it.

Give Each Source a Job

Finding five sources is not the same as building a useful research base.

A source earns its place when it helps you evaluate something specific in the client document.

Example Jobs for Sources

  • One source might provide accessibility criteria.
  • Another might explain plain-language principles.
  • Another might give you a usability-testing method.
  • Another might help you evaluate headings, tables, visuals, or navigation.

The Evidence Toolbox resources are useful because they point to standards, checklists, and methods you can apply directly to the document you’re studying.

As you research, ask: What will this source help me investigate?

Don’t build a pile of sources. Build a set of tools.

Your annotated bibliography will be stronger when every source has a clear purpose in the investigation.
