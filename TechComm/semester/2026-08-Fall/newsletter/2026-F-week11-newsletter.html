I Can Predict Your Report from Your Outline

After years of reading student reports, I can usually predict how well a report will turn out just by looking at its outline. Consider these two approaches:

Still Needs Planning

I. Introduction
II. Methods
III. Results
IV. Conclusions
V. Recommendations

Ready to Develop

III. Results
  1. Navigation Problems
    1. Unclear menu labels
    2. Inconsistent terminology
  2. Accessibility Problems
    1. Missing alternative text
    2. Insufficient color contrast

The second outline shows specific topics and supporting details. I can see what the writer plans to investigate—and that’s a strong sign of a successful report.

Your Outline Is a Diagnostic Tool

Before submitting your outline, look at its shape. Do some sections have several detailed subpoints while others have almost nothing?

That imbalance might reveal something important:

  • Missing information: You may need more research or evidence.
  • Uneven coverage: One issue may be getting too much attention.
  • Organizational problems: Some ideas may belong in different sections.

Don’t automatically add more subpoints just to make the outline look balanced. Figure out why the difference exists.

An Outline Is a Promise, Not a Contract

Your outline should be specific and well-developed, but that doesn’t mean you’re locked into every heading and subheading. It’s a working outline—your best plan based on what you know right now.

As you continue investigating your client document, you might discover a problem you hadn’t anticipated, find that an issue is less significant than you expected, or identify a better way to organize your findings. That’s part of the research and writing process.

Make a thoughtful plan now. Then let your evidence help you improve it.
