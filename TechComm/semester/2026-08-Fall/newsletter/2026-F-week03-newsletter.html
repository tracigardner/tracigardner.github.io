Skim It Now. Know Where to Find It Later.

This week includes two readings that work best as reference resources: Ch 10, Writing Correct and Effective Sentences, and App C, Editing and Proofreading Your Documents.

How to Read These Sources

🧠 Don’t try to memorize everything in them. Skim both so you know what’s there and can return to them when you need help. The goal isn’t to memorize a rulebook. It’s to build a collection of resources and strategies you know how to use when you need them.

In Ch 10, pay particular attention to the plain language strategies. You can put several of them to work immediately as you edit your Correspondence & Informational Report.

Putting the Details into Action

✏️ Try a quick plain-language edit before you submit:

  • Shorten long sentences.
  • Replace jargon with familiar words.
  • Cut unnecessary language.
  • Turn complicated series into lists when appropriate.
  • Replace vague claims with specific details.

🧭Clear Writing Is an Ethical Choice

Professional ethics may sound like something that matters only when a writer faces a major decision.

📝 But ethics also shows up in smaller choices, including how clearly you communicate.

🔎 Accurate information can still be difficult to use if it is buried in jargon, long sentences, or confusing organization.

That’s why plain language is more than a writing style. It’s a way of respecting your reader and making information easier to understand and use.

This matters in your Correspondence & Informational Report. Luci Lee and her team need clear information about accessibility tools so they can make an informed decision.

As you edit, ask yourself: Am I making this as easy as possible for my reader to understand?

Clear writing gives readers meaningful access to information. That’s not just good technical writing. It’s an ethical responsibility.

⚖️Accurate, but Is It Fair?

Suppose everything in your report is technically accurate. Does that automatically make the report objective and ethical?

🤨 Not necessarily.

Writers choose which facts to include, which details to emphasize, and how much attention to give each topic. Those choices can influence readers even when every statement is true.

For example, you might describe Tool A’s capabilities in detail but give Tool B only a few sentences. Or you might list several limitations for one tool while barely mentioning limitations for the other.

Nothing you wrote may be false, but the comparison is not balanced.

Before submitting your report, ask: Did I use similar criteria, comparable detail, and neutral language for both tools?

Ethical technical writing means more than getting the facts right. It means presenting those facts fairly enough for readers to make their own informed decision.
