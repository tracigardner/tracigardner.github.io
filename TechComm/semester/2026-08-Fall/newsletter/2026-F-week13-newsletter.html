Your Table of Contents Is a Reality Check

Your table of contents does more than help readers find information. It gives you a quick way to evaluate your report's organization.

When you can see all your headings together, problems that were easy to overlook while drafting may become obvious.

For example, look at this excerpt from a fictional report's table of contents. What would you change?

The three Results headings describe their topics at different levels of specificity. Two name particular problems, while “Accessibility” names a broad category.

More parallel, descriptive headings would make the organization clearer and help readers understand what the report covers.

Table of Contents (Excerpt)

1. Introduction1
2. Methods3
3. Results5
3.1 Problems with Headings 5
3.2 Navigation Issues 7
3.3 Accessibility 9
4. Conclusions11
5. Recommendations13
Try it: Read only the headings in your table of contents. Do they create a clear, consistent outline of your investigation?

Give Your Future Self a Head Start

You've spent several weeks investigating, organizing, and drafting your Recommendation Report. By the end of this week, aim to have a complete rough draft, including the front and back matter.

Before Thanksgiving break, take a few minutes to leave yourself a revision plan. When you return, you won't have to spend time remembering what still needs attention.

Note to Future Me

When I return to my report, I need to:

  • Replace the placeholder in Results with the annotated screenshot.
  • Check that the executive summary reflects my final recommendations.
  • Update the table of contents and verify the page numbers.

Make your reminders specific. “Fix the report” won't tell you much after the break, but “Add the missing citation for the accessibility guideline” identifies a task you can tackle immediately.
